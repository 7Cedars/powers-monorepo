 

## **1\. Adapting Ostrom for High-Complexity Contexts**

**Huntjens, P., Lebel, L., Pahl-Wostl, C., Camkin, J., Schulze, R., & Kranz, N. (2012). Institutional design propositions for the governance of adaptation to climate change in the water sector. *Global Environmental Change*, *22*, 67-81. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2011.09.015](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2011.09.015)**  
Cited by: 442

* **Summary:** This academic paper builds directly upon Elinor Ostrom’s institutional design principles for common-pool resources. The authors argue that while Ostrom’s principles are foundational, highly complex and uncertain environments (like climate change adaptation) require additional rules. They propose eight refined design propositions that support a "management as learning" approach, emphasising that successful governance rules cannot be blueprinted but must be intricately tuned to local geography, ecology, economies, and cultures.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** Since you mentioned Ostrom, this is an excellent bridge. It takes her foundational theory of local self-governance and demonstrates exactly *how* to modify those rules to accommodate high levels of uncertainty and specific local contexts.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read on Wageningen University Repository](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/institutional-design-propositions-for-the-governance-of-adaptatio) or via [DOI](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2011.09.015).

## **2\. Power, People, and Informal Rules**

**May, C. K. (2022). Complex adaptive governance systems: a framework to understand institutions, organizations, and people in socio-ecological systems. *Socio-Ecological Practice Research*, *4*, 39-54. https://doi.org/10.1007/s42532-021-00101-7**  
Cited by: 81

* **Summary:** This paper introduces the Complex Adaptive Governance Systems Framework (CAGS-F). It provides a methodology for analysing how formal rules (laws, property rights) interact with informal rules (social norms, deep-seated cultural values) across different scales. Crucially, it provides tools to identify how governance structures unintentionally embed power and privilege, and how they can be redesigned to include diverse, context-specific stakeholder groups.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** Effective governance is not just about writing good formal rules; it is about understanding how those rules interact with human behaviour and existing power dynamics. This paper provides a highly structured way to audit and design rules that account for the messy reality of human interaction.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read via PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762444/).

## **3\. Macro-Level Public Administration Design**

**Podger, A., Chan, H. S., & Wanna, J. (2020). DESIGNING GOVERNANCE STRUCTURES FOR PERFORMANCE AND ACCOUNTABILITY. *Designing Governance Structures for Performance and Accountability*, 1-12. [https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctv1bvncsf.7](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctv1bvncsf.7)**  
Cited by: 1

* **Summary:** Published by the Australia and New Zealand School of Government (ANZSOG), this book evaluates how different jurisdictions (specifically Australia, the People's Republic of China, and Taiwan) design formal and informal governance structures to ensure public sector accountability and performance. It explores the tension between political control and bureaucratic autonomy, and how the "purpose" of a system dictates its structural form across vastly different political contexts.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** This provides a strong practitioner and think-tank perspective. It moves away from local resource management into macro-level organisational design, offering comparative case studies that prove how heavily the overarching political and cultural context must dictate the structural design of governance bodies.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Download the full book via ANU Press](https://press.anu.edu.au/publications/series/anzsog/designing-governance-structures-performance-and-accountability).

## **4\. Transactional Governance on the Ground**

**Francis, D., Karalashvili, N., & Murrell, P. (2019). Mapping the Landscape of Transactions: The Governance of Business Relations in Latin America. *SSRN Electronic Journal*. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3378731**  
Cited by: 3

* **Summary:** Produced by researchers associated with the World Bank, this extensive working paper maps how businesses actually govern their relationships in environments where formal legal rules might be weak or slow. By surveying firms across six Latin American countries, the authors reveal how actors combine formal mechanisms (like legally enforceable contracts) with informal mechanisms (like mutual trust or third-party mediation) to create effective "transactional governance structures."  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** When designing governance scripts, it is vital to understand that formal rules often fail if they do not accommodate the informal trust networks already present in a community. This paper provides excellent, data-driven empirical evidence from the Global South on how actors organically build functional governance ecosystems when top-down rules are insufficient.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Download the PDF via the World Bank](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/doingBusiness/pdf/MappingLandscapeOfTransactions_May2020.pdf) or via [SSRN](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id%3D3378731).

## **5\. Iterative Design and Local Capability**

**Andrews, M., Pritchett, L., & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). *World Development*, *51*, 234-244. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2012.11.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2012.11.005)**

* **Summary:** Originating from researchers at the Center for Global Development and Harvard, this paper heavily critiques the practice of importing "best practice" governance blueprints into new contexts (isomorphic mimicry). Instead, it proposes Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA), a framework for building governance capability by focusing on locally nominated problems, encouraging trial and error, and establishing rapid feedback loops.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** This is a cornerstone practitioner-academic piece on why rigid, context-blind rules fail. It provides a robust methodology for structuring governance rules that evolve organically in response to the specific, shifting realities of the community they serve.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read via Harvard University Open Access](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/10986708) or via [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2012.11.005).

## **6\. Institutional Bricolage and Social Embeddedness**

**Cleaver, F. (2002). Reinventing Institutions: Bricolage and the Social Embeddedness of Natural Resource Management. *The European Journal of Development Research*, *14*(2), 11-30. https://doi.org/10.1080/714000425**

* **Summary:** Frances Cleaver challenges the rational-choice institutionalism often associated with Ostrom. She introduces the concept of "institutional bricolage"—the process by which people consciously and unconsciously patch together new governance rules by drawing on existing social norms, cultural symbols, and historical power structures, rather than designing them cleanly from scratch.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** If you are building a script for governance, this piece is a vital sociological counter-weight. It highlights that no set of rules is ever dropped onto a blank slate; effective design must account for how participants will inevitably blend formal rules with their existing informal, cultural practices.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read via ResearchGate / Author copy](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24095400_Reinventing_Institutions_Bricolage_and_the_Social_Embeddedness_of_Natural_Resource_Management) or institutional repositories.

## **7\. The "Democracy Cube" for Participatory Governance**

**Fung, A. (2006). Varieties of Participation in Complex Governance. *Public Administration Review*, *66*(s1), 66-75. [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6210.2006.00667.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6210.2006.00667.x)**

* **Summary:** Archon Fung provides a systematic framework for understanding and designing participatory governance. He creates a three-dimensional "democracy cube" categorising systems based on: who is allowed to participate, how they communicate and make decisions (e.g., voting vs. deliberation), and how much authority those decisions actually carry.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** This paper is highly structural and excellent for a "script-building" mindset. It provides a clear taxonomy of rule-sets, allowing you to explicitly design the dimensions of participation depending on whether the goal is legitimacy, effectiveness, or social justice within a specific context.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read via Harvard Kennedy School Repository](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/3211831).

## **8\. The Concept of "Institutional Fit"**

**Epstein, G., Pittman, J., Alexander, S. M., et al. (2015). Institutional fit and the sustainability of social–ecological systems. *Current Opinion in Environmental Sustainability*, *14*, 34-40. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cosust.2015.03.005**

* **Summary:** This paper unpacks the concept of "institutional fit," which argues that governance rules will fail unless they perfectly align with the spatial, temporal, and functional scales of the system they are trying to manage. It explores how a mismatch (e.g., short-term political rules trying to govern long-term ecological or social cycles) leads to systemic failure.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** It provides a diagnostic tool for context. Before writing a rule, this framework forces you to ask if the scope and timing of the rule actually match the reality of the problem being governed.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read via the University of Waterloo Repository](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/10334) or via [DOI](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cosust.2015.03.005).

## **9\. Systems Thinking in Public Policy**

**OECD (2020). *Systemic Thinking for Policy Making: The Potential of Systems Analysis for Addressing Global Policy Challenges in the 21st Century*. OECD Publishing, Paris.**

* **Summary:** This major report from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) is a practitioner’s guide to moving away from linear, predictable governance models. It details how public administration can adopt systems thinking to manage complex, overlapping challenges, emphasising the need for rules that allow for continuous adaptation rather than rigid adherence to a pre-set plan.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** A premier think-tank perspective. It translates abstract complexity theory into actionable public sector governance, detailing how state and municipal actors actually attempt to write flexible rules for unpredictable contexts.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read or download via the OECD iLibrary](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/governance/systemic-thinking-for-policy-making_879c4f7a-en).

## **10\. Formalising Polycentricity**

**Carlisle, K., & Gruby, R. L. (2019). Polycentric Systems of Governance: A Theoretical Model for the Commons. *Policy Studies Journal*, *47*(4), 927-952. https://doi.org/10.1111/psj.12212**

* **Summary:** Taking Ostrom’s concept of polycentricity (multiple overlapping governing authorities) out of specific case studies, this paper builds a formal theoretical model. It identifies the specific structural properties and mechanisms—such as conflict resolution and institutional linkages—that must be present for a decentralised, multi-layered governance system to function effectively rather than collapsing into chaos.  
* **Reason for Inclusion:** If your script needs to define how different autonomous groups or committees interact without a central boss, this paper breaks down the exact mechanisms and rules required to keep a polycentric system stable and coordinated.  
* **Open Access Link:** [Read via Colorado State University Repository](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://mountainscholar.org/handle/10217/194729) or via [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/psj.12212).
